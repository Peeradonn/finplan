## 1. The whole talk in one minute

**The question (slide 1):** What makes a plan sustainable?

**The easy answer, and why it fails:** "Put 20–25% in ESG funds." Then the share becomes the target. A label says
yes or no; it can't tell you what the money does. And a label is not a check: one global ESG fund on the SFC's own ESG
list reported US$0.00 of assets at all 23 filings from Sep 2024 to Sep 2026 (slide 10). The list never said it had
assets; checking a product is the adviser's job. That fund is the hook, not the proof of "beyond labels": the proof is
the climate index fund (a footprint, not a change) and the 47% of the downturn's loss no fund label looks at.

**Our answer (the thesis, word for word):**

> **A plan is sustainable if it holds its limits when climate hits, claims only what it changes, and pays for values
> only from the room above its limits.**

Three tests, true for any client:

| Test | In plain words | Which way it looks | The Wongs' proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The limit** | The family's money must still last when climate hits everything they own and earn, not just the funds | Outside in: what climate does to them | In one disorderly 2030–31 downturn, the money still lasts in 90 futures in 100, above their limit of 85 (slide 5) |
| **The change** | Count only what the money really changes in the world, against what would happen anyway | Inside out: what they do to the world | Up to 134 t CO2e a year, all from the business; the funds: "a footprint, not a change", nothing claimed (slides 7, 12) |
| **The room** | Values are paid for only from the money above the limit, priced in HK$ | The values budget | HK$1.7M of room; the scholarship uses HK$0.74M, Carmen's ESG funds HK$0.28M, ≈HK$0.72M still free (slide 4) |

**How we run the test: PRISM** (slide 2). Five steps; the steps are any planner's, and we say so.

| Step | The question | What it does | Test it checks | Slides |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P**rofile | Who decides, within what limits? | Each decision-maker asked alone; the limit and the room set before any product | Limit, room | 3, 4 |
| **R**isk | What can climate do to their money? | Everything they own and earn, in one shock | Limit | 5, 6 |
| **I**mpact | What does their money change? | Only changes we can show, not a footprint | Change | 7 |
| **S**elect | Which options pass? | Each decision gets two prices, then one of four verdicts | All three | 8–11 |
| **M**onitor | When do we decide again? | The plan's own label, read every year | All three | 12 |

**What's ours** (slide 2, left): the limit on *everything* they own (home, business, savings, job), not just the funds;
the change counted only where we can show it; values priced in HK$ from a room the limit sizes; every verdict comes with
the number that would flip it (its switch point).

**The answer for the Wongs** (slides 1 and 13): electric vans on a plain loan; recycled plastic at today's price; a
climate index fund at the plain fund's fee; the flat's route checked against a price gap every year; a scholarship
funded for life now; three green products refused (green mortgage, green deposit, sustainability-linked loan).
**Capital:** HK$0.74M the family's (the scholarship fund); HK$1.91M of equipment and ≈HK$0.27M of working capital, the
business's. **Outcomes:** +HK$1.05M for the children against the same plan without the test; up to 134 t CO2e a year
less, if verified and delivered.

## 2. The four verdicts (the close, slide 13)

Every decision gets two prices: **for the children** (what they inherit at Carmen's 89) and **for the world** (a real
change shown, or not). First, though, the limit: **a choice that breaks it is out, however green.**

| | Real change shown | No real change shown |
|---|---|---|
| **Adds, or costs about nothing** | **Do it.** Electric vans, LED, air-con (+HK$0.51M, 26 t a year); recycled plastic (≈HK$0 at today's resin price, 108 t) | **Do it, but don't call it green.** Customer carbon data (−HK$0.09M; benefit unquantified); the flat's route, checked yearly; the climate index fund (its plain twin's fee; a footprint) |
| **Costs money** | **Pay knowingly, from the room.** Fund the scholarship now (−HK$1.32M for the children; HK$0.74M given now, for life) | **Don't pay for the label.** Green mortgage, green deposit, sustainability-linked loan; the green bond fund and the MPF low-carbon fund (−HK$0.62M; no impact claimed) |

**The trap in the bottom-right corner.** A judge will ask: "You say don't pay for the label, yet the family pays
HK$0.62M for the green bond and MPF funds?" The answer: the corner says what the rule recommends and what may be
claimed. Those funds cost money and show no change we can measure, so we would never sell them as impact. They're
bought for the family's values, from the room, knowing the price, and we claim nothing for them. "We don't refuse
Carmen's values. We refuse the label."

**Know this before a judge spots it:** slides 11 and 13 say "her green bond, MPF funds", but half of *each* parent's
MPF is in the low-carbon fund, so about HK$1.9K a year of the cost is Adrian's, and it hasn't been tested against his
0.5% limit. Win has the answer (`04_win.md`, Q19); Pete and Win decide before Saturday whether his half moves to a
plain fund.

## 3. The three comparisons (the most confusing part: learn this table)

We compare against three different things, each for one job. Never mix them in one answer without naming which.

| Compared with | What it is | The question it answers | Where | The number |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Plain choices** | Plain funds, no giving, no vans | How much may values cost? (the room) | Slide 4 | Room HK$1.7M |
| **Each decision's plain alternative** | The plain twin fund; new diesel vans; no carbon data; downsizing vs the reverse mortgage | Which corner does this decision land in? | Slide 13; backup 22 | e.g. the vans +HK$0.51M |
| **The reasonable planner's plan** | The same careful household plan, labelled ESG funds as cheap as ours (0.20%), the gift pledged yearly, no climate check on the home or business | What did the framework change? (the headline) | Slides 1, 13; backup 15 | +HK$1.05M |

**The headline, +HK$1.05M, split honestly** (backup 15):
- the vans **+HK$0.54M**: the framework's part (only a test of the business finds them);
- funding the gift now instead of pledging it every year **+HK$0.39M**: ordinary good planning a tax-aware planner might also do;
- fees **+HK$0.12M**: ordinary planning.
- In the downturn: +HK$1.03M.

Say this split **first** if anyone asks where the HK$1.05M comes from. Against a planner who also buys the vans and
funds the gift, the money gain is about zero (our reading, not a separate model run). What the test then adds is what
money doesn't show: the tonnes at no cost, the flat's switch point, the guard on the customers' carbon questions, and
what may be claimed.

**The reasonable planner's plan is not a strawman.** It passes both limits: 95 in 100 normally, 86 in the downturn
(ours: 97 and 90). It is defined by what it does, as our own advice without the test. It is **not** a claim about how
other advisers work. Never say it "falls below the floor".

**Three numbers that look different but are the same thing** (a CPA's favourite trap):

| Looks like a clash | Why both are right |
|---|---|
| Scholarship: HK$0.74M (slide 4) and −HK$1.32M (slide 13) | HK$0.74M is the fund given now. −HK$1.32M is what the children inherit at Carmen's 89 without it, in today's money: money given away no longer grows |
| The ESG funds that cost money: HK$0.28M (slide 4) and −HK$0.62M (slide 11) | The same choice (the green bond fund and the MPF low-carbon fund, HK$5.1K a year in fees) in two units: today's giving vs the children's inheritance at 89 |
| The vans: +HK$0.51M (slides 9, 13) and +HK$0.54M (backup 15) | +0.51 is the vans alone against new diesel vans. +0.54 is the vans' step inside the headline's sequence |

## 5. How to read our numbers

**The model:** 10,000 simulated futures; we quote the **median**, in **today's HK$**, of what the children inherit at
Carmen's 89 (including the flat). "97 in 100" means the money lasts to Carmen's 89 in 97 of 100 futures. **Every odds
figure assumes Adrian works to 65** (slide 12 says so).

**Every number carries a tag:**

| Tag | Means | How to say it |
|---|---|---|
| MEASURED | Our model's output | "Our model, 10,000 futures, the median" |
| SOURCED | A named, dated source | "BlackRock and MSCI, October 2026" |
| ESTIMATED | Our arithmetic on sourced inputs | "Our estimate, from UK government emission factors" |
| ILLUSTRATIVE | Our own assumption | "We set that. Here's the number where the verdict would flip" |

"Illustrative" is not a weakness if you say it first and give the switch point. Hiding it is the weakness.

## 7. Questions any of us may get (20-second answers)

Answer first, then the reason, then the source. Stop. If it goes deeper, hand over (§8).

**"Your framework in one sentence?"** A plan is sustainable if it holds its limits when climate hits, claims only what
it changes, and pays for values only from the room above its limits. PRISM is how we check it.

**"Isn't this what every good planner does?"** The steps are, and slide 2 says so. What's ours: the limit set on
everything the family owns before any product, values paid only from the room above it and priced in HK$, and every
verdict shown with the number that flips it. On the Wongs, that reversed advice that isn't about green products at
all: the flat's route (slide 6). *(Deep version: Pete.)*

**"Your 'reasonable planner' is a strawman."** It passes both limits (95 and 86) and uses ESG funds as cheap as ours.
Of our +HK$1.05M, about half is the vans; the rest is ordinary planning, and we say so. Against a planner who also
bought the vans and funded the gift, the money gain is about zero; the test still adds the flat's switch point, the
tonnes and what may be claimed. *(Backup 15.)*

**"Why 95 and 85?"** They're the family's limits as we set them: illustrative. Every verdict comes with the number that
flips it (backup 25), and the family can lower a limit only explicitly, both parents signing.

**"What if equities return less than 6%?"** The plan holds 95 down to about 5.3% a year, and 85 in the downturn down to
about 5.2%. The room shrinks fast: HK$0.94M at 5.5%, HK$0.15M at 5%. That's why the room is measured again every year,
and values spend only what's there. *(Backup 26.)*

**"Is the plan climate-resilient?"** We don't claim that. Nothing in it hedges a market fall. In the downturn it still
lasts in 90 futures in 100, above the limit of 85, and it cuts the routes it can: the wrong exit for the flat, and
the customers' carbon questions.

**"What would you not do?"** Pay for a green mortgage (at most HK$1,000 once, and the building isn't rated); pay for a
green-loan label (≈HK$12K saved, ≈HK$12K review); a sustainability-linked loan at her size; the fund that reported
zero assets; a clean-energy fund that breaks Adrian's limit; a green deposit with no rule on what the bank finances;
any impact claim for listed shares.

**"Isn't PRISM just double materiality?"** Yes, built on it on purpose (slide 2's footer). What we add is applying it to a
family's whole balance sheet, with decision rules and prices in HK$.

**"What if Adrian loses his job?"** It's the plan's biggest risk, and it isn't a climate risk. Every odds figure assumes
he works to 65. With no response, the plan falls to 44 in 100. The response that restores both limits is on backup
19: half his pay again, less spending, and Carmen working to 65, giving 96 and 87.

**"You just reused the competition's family."** They're our client; the topic asked us to define every figure
ourselves. A distributor is representative: Hong Kong has about 12 import, export and wholesale firms for every
manufacturer (TID, June 2025).

**"26.6% is above their 20–25% ask: did you overshoot?"** Nobody targeted it. Adrian's limit stops his climate fund at
24%; Carmen's limit covers all her shares. The share is what the limits produced. *(Deep version: Win.)*

**"What are the measured sustainability outcomes?"** None are measured yet, and we say so. The 134 t are estimated and
measured from 2027 against a carbon baseline. The scholarship's students are counted. The funds: a footprint, with no
impact claimed.

## 8. How we run the 10 minutes of questions

- **Who answers:** the person the judge asks. If nobody is named, the owner of that slide answers. Pete picks up
  anything about the framework, the headline or the family, and routes the rest.

  | Topic | Owner | Slides | Backups |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Framework, profiles, the limit and the room, the headline, governance | Pete | 1–4, 13 | 15, 22, 24–28, 33 |
  | Climate risk, the downturn, the flat, insurance, Adrian's job | Lookbua | 5, 6 | 16–19, 23 |
  | The business: footprint, cuts, funding, green loans | Fahtai | 7–9 | 20, 29–31 |
  | The portfolio, the products, the dashboard | Win | 10–12 | 21, 32 |

- **The shape of an answer:** one sentence that answers (yes, no, or the number), then the reason, then the source or
  tag. Stop at about 30 seconds. Aim for five to seven good answers in ten minutes, not two long ones.
- **Handing over:** "Fahtai ran those numbers: Fahtai?" The person who handed over adds at most one sentence, and only
  to correct a number.
- **Showing a backup:** say "Backup 25 shows it"; the person at the laptop types the number and presses Enter (slide 34
  is the index). *Proposal: Lookbua runs the laptop during questions, since Lookbua also owns logistics.*
- **If we don't know:** "We didn't test that. Here's how we would: …" Never invent a number. A judge forgives a gap
  far faster than a figure that falls apart.
- **If a judge is right:** agree with the true part first, then say what it changes (often: which corner, not the rule).
- **If a judge is wrong:** don't argue. Give the number and its source, and point to the slide.
